Instructor guide: what to look for when evaluating student applications

Author

Thaís Paiva Galletti - Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais (UFMG), Brazil
(thaispaiva@est.ufmg.br)

Part 1 — Exploratory Analysis

A strong submission shows:

  • Frequency computed correctly as claims per policy-year (total claims divided by total exposure within each group), not claims per policy. This is the single most important technical point in the EDA: with a semester window, no policy has more than half a year of exposure (the average is about a third of a year), so claims per policy understate the annual frequency by a factor of about three (roughly 0.08 claims per policy against 0.25 per policy-year).
  • Severity restricted to policies with at least one claim, typically displayed on a log scale given the heavy right skew.
  • Frequency and severity broken down by the main rating factors (sex, age group, region), with sensible grouping (age bands rather than raw age; regions with very few policies handled or filtered).
  • Some look at the observed premium (pre_casco) against the same factors, connecting the data to the pricing question.

Typical expected patterns (useful as a sanity check while grading): higher frequency for younger drivers (peaking in the 26 to 35 band), small differences in frequency by sex with somewhat higher severity for men, substantial variation across regions, and right-skewed severity with a long tail.

Part 2 — Model

Both options are equally valid, and within Option A the choice of distributions is left to the students: grade the execution and the justification, not the choice itself.

Option A (two-part model):

  • log(exposicao) used as offset in the frequency model. A model without the offset is a substantive error.
  • A sensible, justified distribution for each part: for counts, Poisson is the natural choice (negative binomial is a fine alternative if overdispersion is raised); for severity, a positive right-skewed distribution such as Gamma or log-normal. What matters is that the team can defend the choice.
  • Severity model fit on average claim amount (valor_sinistro / n_sinistros), restricted to the claims subset. Fitting a Gamma to the total claim amount including zeros is a common error (Gamma does not admit zeros; students sometimes “fix” this by adding a constant, which should be flagged).
  • Pure premium correctly formed as the product of the two predictions.

Option B (Tweedie):

  • log(exposicao) as offset, power parameter between 1 and 2 (estimated or fixed with justification).
  • Pure premium taken directly from the prediction.

Both options:

  • The four required variables (sex, age, region, model year) included.
  • A coefficient table with interpretation of sign and magnitude (ideally via exp(coef), read as multiplicative effects on the premium). Interpretation should be consistent with the EDA findings; a mismatch that goes uncommented suggests the team did not connect the two parts.
  • Formal variable-selection procedures (stepwise AIC, likelihood-ratio testing) are deliberately out of scope. Do not reward or penalize their presence; the focus is interpretation.

Part 3 — Premium Calculator

A strong calculator:

  • Includes all five required inputs (sex, age, region, model year, bonus class) and returns the pure premium for the selected profile.
  • Shows at least one premium response curve (premium as a function of one variable, others held fixed), correctly recomputed when inputs change.
  • Handles profiles outside the training data gracefully. This is the most revealing quality test: select a rare region or an extreme age and see whether the app fails with an error, silently returns nonsense, or shows an informative message. Graceful handling deserves credit.

Application quality overall also covers: the app runs without errors from a clean session, the three sections are complete and navigable, and outputs are labeled well enough to be read by someone who did not build the app.

Common pitfalls to watch for

  1. Factor-level mismatch between the frequency and severity models. The severity model sees only the claims subset, which typically lacks some factor levels (small regions in particular). If levels are not aligned (e.g., with droplevels() and consistent level sets at prediction time), the calculator fails, sometimes silently, for certain profiles. Test the calculator with a few uncommon regions during grading.
  2. Frequency per policy instead of per policy-year (missing exposure in the EDA and/or missing offset in the model).
  3. Gamma model fit on total claim amount including zeros, or on valor_sinistro without dividing by n_sinistros.
  4. The utilizacao = 0 category ignored silently. Teams that notice it and make an explicit choice (keep as “not informed” level, or exclude with justification) show the kind of data awareness this case is designed to teach.
  5. Extrapolation presented without caveats, e.g., a smooth premium curve extended over ages or regions where the model has little or no data.

Presentation / communication

Look for: a clear statement of which modeling option was chosen and why, correct reading of at least a few coefficients in business terms (“drivers in region X pay about Y% more, holding everything else fixed”), and honest acknowledgment of limitations.

If AI tools were used, the required declaration should be present, and the team should be able to explain any modeling choice when questioned. Inability to justify a choice that appears in their own app is the clearest signal of uncritical AI use and should be reflected in this criterion.
